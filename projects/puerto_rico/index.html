Building Back, Pushing Out: How Climate Adaptation Drives Displacement in Puerto Rico

Climate and Displacement · Northeastern University Senior Capstone · 2025 · Qualitative Research

Introduction

The reality of living through a natural disaster is one many people cannot fathom. In 2025, climate change is no longer an abstract concern, but a daily reality felt across communities. Fortunately, many nations and individuals are taking proactive measures, implementing new policies and practices aimed at mitigating the harmful effects of climate change and preventing further degradation of our shared environment (United Nations).

While these actions are often praised, we rarely stop to consider the unintended consequences they may bring. Climate adaptation, defined by The Global Center on Adaptation as “taking action to prepare for and adjust to the current and projected impacts of climate change,” can itself create new issues for communities facing it (Global Center on Adaptation). This paper focuses on Puerto Rico, an island continuously ravaged by natural disasters and increasingly worried about the effects of climate change.

Specifically, I argue that climate adaptation efforts on the island are aiding in displacing the local population, both through forced migration and more subtle forms of cultural and social erosion. Additionally, focusing on the municipality of Rincón provides further evidence of this issue. In this context, the term displacement refers to both the forced migration of individuals from their homes (literal house and neighborhoods), as well as voluntary displacement due to their home no longer representing the culture they once knew.

Results/Conclusion

    To ensure climate adaptation benefits the people it is meant to protect, future plans must center community voices from the beginning and prioritize justice, equity, and community leadership. I emphasize four ways to achieve this, as follows;

  • Create transparent forums for local engagement from the beginning ensures that adaptation efforts are shaped by the lived experiences of local residents.
  • Invest in cultural preservation such as funding community spaces, protecting historically significant neighborhoods, and supporting local language, arts, and traditions helps to maintain the identity and vibrancy of Puerto Rican communities (EESI).
  • Anti-displacement policies are crucial to make adaptation equitable (Dorazio). Tools such as rent control can keep housing costs stable as neighborhoods improve. Housing stipends or subsidies also can help families facing financial instability stay rooted in their communities rather than being forced to move.
  • Community land trusts, where land is held collectively by a nonprofit or community group, can ensure long-term affordability and prevent speculative real estate development (Georgetown Climate Center). These models shift the focus from private profit to public good.

    • Together, these measures offer a blueprint for climate resilience that is socially sustainable. They allow Puerto Rican communities to adapt not only to environmental threats, but also to the economic and social pressures that often come with adaptation efforts. In doing so, they preserve the right of residents to remain in place and stay connected to their homes, neighborhoods, and heritage. True resilience lies not just in withstanding the effects of climate change, but in ensuring that the people who live through it are still there to rebuild, thrive, and carry on their ways of life.
